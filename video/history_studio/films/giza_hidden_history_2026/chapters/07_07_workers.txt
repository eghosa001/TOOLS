The Workers' Lost City and the People We Almost Forgot

If you were standing beside the Great Pyramid while it was being built, the noise would not come only from the construction site. You would hear tools striking stone, boats unloading cargo, animals moving through enclosures and food being prepared for teams of people. A massive building project has a soundscape of ordinary work. It also has a geography. Workers need somewhere to sleep, wash, eat and receive instructions. Archaeologists have spent decades searching for evidence of those facilities, because they tell us as much about how the pyramids were built as measurements of the monuments themselves.

South of the Sphinx lies the area called Heit el-Ghurab, often translated as the Wall of the Crow. Ancient Egypt Research Associates, or AERA, has investigated a large settlement there. Excavations uncovered galleries interpreted as housing or barracks, bakeries, spaces associated with livestock, officials' residences and administrative buildings. Archaeologists study pottery, animal bones, charred grains and discarded stone flakes. To a film viewer, those things may seem less dramatic than a secret tunnel. To a historian, they can reveal how a civilisation fed and organised thousands of people.

Food is a particularly revealing form of evidence. A large workforce cannot survive on royal ambition. Archaeologists investigate cattle and other animal remains, bread production, grain and the infrastructure of provisioning. Diet can tell us about supply networks and social differences. It also reveals the power of the state to gather resources from many communities and deliver them to a concentrated site. The operation was more like a large temporary urban economy than the simple picture of endless lines of anonymous slaves shown in old films.

That brings us to one of the most stubborn popular questions: did enslaved people build the pyramids? The answer is more complicated than either dramatic stereotype or convenient slogan. Evidence for organised workers, specialist teams, burial practices and the provision of food challenges the image that every builder was a chained prisoner. But the surviving evidence does not prove that every worker volunteered freely or that forms of compulsory labour were absent. Ancient states could draw on workers under a range of obligations. Historians distinguish trained craftspeople, conscripted labour and other social positions instead of flattening them all into one category.

Archaeologists have also studied cemeteries associated with workers near Giza. Human remains provide clues about physical strain, injury and care. Healed fractures suggest that at least some people survived accidents and received support during recovery. These findings do not mean working conditions were safe by modern standards. A large construction site with heavy stone would have involved severe risks. They do complicate the idea that the workforce was treated only as disposable labour. Human bones are records of both danger and survival.

It is important to keep the dating straight. The major excavated settlement phases at Heit el-Ghurab are especially associated with later Fourth Dynasty projects, including the reigns of Khafre and Menkaure. We should not make every uncovered wall into Khufu's personal kitchen. However, the settlement gives direct evidence of the types of logistics and organisation that the Giza pyramid-building economy could support. Alongside contemporary inscriptions and workers' marks, it transforms our picture of the people behind the stone.

Perhaps the most poignant evidence is a name. Ancient work gangs sometimes had identities recorded in inscriptions. The workers were not all invisible. They belonged to teams, and some left marks tied to royal projects. Imagine that a person spent years cutting or moving stone, and the most enduring trace of their life is a mark in a place tourists cannot see. We cannot invent that person's thoughts, but we can treat their trace as more than a footnote to the king's biography.

The workforce also included administrators who may never have lifted a block. Someone calculated supplies, organised shifts, maintained vessels, repaired tools and dealt with disputes. Ship crews operated beyond the plateau. Quarry workers were stationed near rock sources. Craftspeople made and maintained the equipment. There were probably people whose jobs changed as construction advanced. None of this requires imagining a modern corporation in ancient clothing. It requires recognising a complex society using the practical systems available to it.

One of the best ways to understand the pyramid is to turn the camera away from the summit and toward the ground. Look at the oven where bread was baked. Look at animal bones left after meals. Look at the narrow spaces where people slept. Each everyday object says that monumental architecture depended on human lives, skill and routine. And after recognising the workers, we are ready to examine the hardest physical question: how did they move the stone upward?
