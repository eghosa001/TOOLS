## 01 — A book no one could open

[Cold open. No music for the first sentence.]

Imagine owning a book that no living person could open. You can hold it. You know there is writing inside. But the moment you try to read it, the pages begin to fall apart. Now imagine that the book was buried by a volcano almost two thousand years ago, and that it may contain words no one has read since the Roman world was alive.

This is not a legend. Hundreds of carbonized papyrus rolls survive from Herculaneum, a town destroyed in the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in the year 79. For centuries, scholars have faced a brutal choice: leave the scrolls closed, or risk destroying them to find out what they say.

In June 2026, that choice changed. Researchers announced that they had read the entire surviving rolled portion of one ancient scroll without physically unrolling it. And what emerged was not a prophecy, a treasure map or a secret weapon. It was something more human: a serious argument about how people should live.

## 02 — The city in Vesuvius's shadow

Herculaneum stood beside the Bay of Naples, close to the much better-known city of Pompeii. In the first century, its houses, streets and villas formed part of a prosperous Roman coastal world. People traded, bathed, ate, argued, borrowed books and went about ordinary lives in the shadow of Vesuvius.

The eruption in 79 changed that world. Herculaneum was overwhelmed by searing volcanic surges. It is easy to imagine every fragile object being burned to dust. Yet the same disaster preserved things that usually disappear from the archaeological record: wood, food, furniture, and papyrus.

The heat carbonized the papyrus rather than leaving the entire collection to rot away. Later burial sealed the remains beneath a deep blanket of volcanic material. The books survived, but in a terrible condition. Their writing was trapped inside black, brittle rolls with layers fused and crushed together.

When we say the volcano preserved this library, we do not mean it saved the books unharmed. It destroyed much of what made them readable, even as it kept their material from vanishing altogether. That contradiction is the heart of the story.

## 03 — A private library beneath the ground

The collection came from an extraordinary Roman residence now called the Villa of the Papyri. Its design, gardens, sculpture and scale make clear that it belonged to someone with considerable wealth. It is often associated with Lucius Calpurnius Piso Caesoninus, the father-in-law of Julius Caesar, although the ownership is not conclusively established.

The villa contained a large philosophical library. Many of the surviving works were written by, or connected with, Philodemus of Gadara, an Epicurean philosopher who lived in the first century before our era. That connection matters. It reminds us that a Roman aristocrat's library could preserve Greek intellectual life — debates over knowledge, poetry, the gods, desire and the good life.

We should also remember what we do not know. This is one surviving collection, not a complete record of every book Romans read. And a great deal of the villa remains archaeologically inaccessible. It would be exciting to discover another room of manuscripts. But a possible undiscovered library is not the same thing as a library already found.

## 04 — The eighteenth-century discovery

People began investigating the buried towns around Vesuvius during the eighteenth century. In the early 1750s, workers excavating the Villa of the Papyri brought up objects that did not look much like books. Some resembled lumps of charcoal, narrow black sticks that could easily be mistaken for fuel.

They were papyrus rolls. The Herculaneum Society reports about eighteen hundred excavated papyrus fragments, perhaps representing roughly eight hundred original volumes. A fragment is not necessarily a separate book, which is why the two numbers must never be treated as interchangeable.

The discovery was extraordinary: a substantial ancient library, preserved in its archaeological setting. But it also introduced a new problem. The rolls were fragile, and their layers had been compressed into shapes that no reader could simply unfold. Some were damaged beyond recovery by the very methods used to investigate them.

A library had been found. Recovering the words would prove much harder.

## 05 — When reading meant destruction

Early researchers tried ways to get inside. In some cases, the scrolls were cut, peeled or scraped. A layer might yield a few lines of writing, only to be removed to reach another layer underneath. The procedure could recover information, but it could also destroy evidence that later technology might have read better.

During the 1750s, the priest and inventor Antonio Piaggio developed a mechanical apparatus to help unroll papyri gradually. It was a remarkable effort for its time. However, the instrument could not eliminate the basic danger: once a carbonized sheet cracks or separates, its arrangement and its text can be lost.

We must not judge every early scholar as careless. They were working with the methods and materials available to them. But the physical evidence shows the cost of opening these objects. One of the great challenges for modern science was to stop treating the scroll like a normal book.

## 06 — The words we could already read

Not every Herculaneum papyrus remained sealed. Scholars painstakingly studied text from opened rolls and fragments. The results changed what we know about ancient philosophy, especially the writings of Philodemus and the Epicurean tradition.

Epicureanism is often misunderstood as a philosophy of endless luxury. In the ancient texts, its treatment of pleasure is much more demanding. It asks what pleasures are worth seeking, what desires create needless anxiety, and how reasoning might help people live well.

There is a quiet irony here. The Herculaneum scrolls have sometimes been advertised online as containing supernatural mysteries. Much of the evidence instead leads us into ordinary human questions. What should we want? What is worth fearing? How do we understand happiness?

The unopened rolls might hold works that are entirely unknown to us. But no one can responsibly say that a particular lost masterpiece is inside until the writing has actually been recovered and identified.

## 07 — The challenge of black ink on black papyrus

Why could researchers not simply point a powerful scanner at the scroll and read through it? Because the problem was never just seeing inside. It was recognizing the ink.

Papyrus contains carbon. Much of the ancient ink also relied on carbon. After the eruption, the page and the lettering could produce very similar signals. An X-ray scan might show the shape of the wound sheets in remarkable detail, while leaving the words almost invisible.

Think of writing with a black pencil on a black sheet of paper, then crushing the paper into a tight spiral. A machine might locate each fold and tear. That does not mean it can easily separate the pencil marks from the sheet itself.

The solution would require several kinds of expertise: powerful imaging, mathematics to locate the surfaces, software to flatten them, machine learning to identify likely ink, and papyrologists who could test whether the marks formed meaningful ancient Greek.

## 08 — A scroll becomes a three-dimensional puzzle

The imaging stage begins with high-resolution X-ray tomography. Instead of taking a single flat photograph, researchers build a three-dimensional picture of the scroll's interior. In a cross-section, the papyrus appears as crowded, twisted layers. Some curl neatly. Others buckle, tear or press against their neighbors.

An unopened scroll does not lie inside the scanner like a tidy roll of modern paper. It is often warped beyond recognition. Before the words can be read, researchers must trace the sheet through that maze. The task is called segmentation: identifying which surfaces belong to the papyrus and how they connect.

Only then can software flatten a selected surface into something like a page. Even at that point, the text may remain difficult to distinguish. Virtual unwrapping is therefore not a single magic button. It is a sequence of steps, and each step can introduce errors if handled carelessly.

## 09 — How AI finds writing

Machine learning enters where faint differences are too difficult to detect consistently by eye. Researchers train models to recognize patterns associated with ink. Some methods exploit subtle changes in the papyrus surface, including textures or deformations linked to ancient writing. Others use improvements in scan resolution that make the ink signal easier to separate.

That does not mean an AI model can reliably invent a missing sentence and call it a discovery. Quite the opposite. The central question is whether a proposed letter is really present in the physical evidence.

A model may suggest shapes that resemble Greek characters. Researchers then compare those shapes with the scanned surface, the known writing system, neighboring marks, and the readings of experts. The goal is not a smooth-looking paragraph. It is a defensible reading that other scholars can examine and challenge.

## 10 — The competition that accelerated everything

In March 2023, Nat Friedman, Daniel Gross and Brent Seales launched the Vesuvius Challenge, an open scientific competition built around the problem of reading unopened Herculaneum scrolls. Imaging data and tools were shared so that teams across the world could attack different parts of the problem.

The prize structure attracted computer scientists, students, engineers and researchers who might never otherwise have worked on papyri. In October 2023, Luke Farritor was recognized for finding the first word inside a still-rolled scroll: an ancient Greek word meaning purple.

That single word mattered because it showed the signal was not just noise. Someone had found a real piece of writing concealed within the carbonized layers. It was a proof that the larger task might be possible.

It was not yet the recovery of an entire book. The distinction is important, because the next years brought several separate breakthroughs that are sometimes blurred together in headlines.

## 11 — The first continuous passages

On 5 February 2024, the Vesuvius Challenge announced its 2023 Grand Prize winners: Youssef Nader, Luke Farritor and Julian Schilliger. Their combined work produced substantial connected text from the unopened roll known as PHerc. Paris. 4.

The newly recovered Greek discussed pleasure, food, music and the senses. One line challenged the idea that something is necessarily more pleasurable simply because it is scarce. Scholars saw a philosophical argument, probably within the Epicurean tradition, rather than a collection of disconnected words.

The achievement was enormous. But at that stage, researchers had read only a small part of that scroll. Claims that the whole library had suddenly been decoded were wrong. The breakthrough showed that virtual unwrapping could reveal continuous ancient thought. Much harder work still lay ahead.

## 12 — Oxford's sealed scroll

Another milestone came in February 2025. The Bodleian Libraries at Oxford announced that researchers had generated a readable image from inside PHerc. 172, one of the Herculaneum scrolls in its collection.

The papyrus had been scanned at Diamond Light Source in England. Its ink was unusually visible in the X-ray data. Researchers suggested that a denser ingredient might have contributed to the difference, but the precise explanation was not yet settled at the time.

The project did not stop at finding a few lines. By June 2026, researchers had recovered more than seventy columns from PHerc. 172 and identified the work as Philodemus's *On Vices*, Book One. A title that had been trapped inside a closed roll could finally be matched to a known ancient author.

## 13 — June 2026: the major breakthrough

Then came 25 June 2026. The Vesuvius Challenge announced the complete virtual unwrapping of the surviving rolled portion of PHerc. 1667, a scroll held in Naples. More than two hundred years of attempts to open that particular object had already caused losses. Its remaining core was narrow: roughly eight centimeters high, where the original roll had been significantly taller.

The researchers nevertheless recovered a continuous sequence of roughly twenty-two surviving columns, laid out across about 1.4 meters of papyrus. This was not a complete pristine ancient book. It was the complete surviving portion that could now be digitally unrolled, its damaged sections and gaps still visible.

For the first time, scholars could follow that preserved text from one end of the surviving roll to the other. An ancient work ceased to be only a physical object and became a connected argument that a reader could study.

## 14 — What did PHerc. 1667 actually say?

The subject was ethics. Its language explored human nature, reason, impulse and moral development. Researchers judged it likely to be a Stoic work, a striking contrast with the large number of Epicurean texts long associated with the Herculaneum collection.

One surviving passage suggests that abandoning our own nature prevents us from grasping what we seek to understand. Another links strenuous inquiry and learning with practical wisdom. These are partial, translated passages, not a complete modern English edition of the original book.

Near the end of the surviving text appears the name Aristocreon, a relative and student of the Stoic philosopher Chrysippus. That reference, together with the style and subjects, supports a date in the second century before our era. It does not yet establish the author's identity beyond doubt.

That is what makes the discovery exciting. A philosophical voice, never available to modern readers in this form, has become accessible — and scholars can now examine what it really argues.

## 15 — Three scrolls, three different discoveries

To understand how fast the field is changing, consider three objects separately.

PHerc. Paris. 4 provided early continuous passages in 2023 and 2024, dealing with pleasure. Later, improved high-resolution imaging allowed researchers to verify the ink in three dimensions against the earlier reading.

PHerc. 1667 yielded the surviving portion of what appears to be a Stoic ethical treatise, digitally unwrapped from end to end in 2026.

PHerc. 139 revealed an author and title: Philodemus, *On Gods*, Book Eight. A title can matter enormously even before the entire body of a text is recovered. It tells historians which work they are investigating and can reshape their understanding of an ancient author's intellectual output.

These are separate manuscripts and separate milestones. Mixing their titles, images or passages would create a misleading documentary, even if every individual photograph were genuine.

## 16 — The machines did not replace the scholars

The headline often says that artificial intelligence read a Roman scroll. That is a convenient shorthand, but it hides the most interesting part of the achievement.

Engineers used imaging systems capable of producing extraordinary detail. Computer scientists mapped distorted sheets and searched for faint ink. Papyrologists examined the resulting letters, read the ancient Greek, evaluated gaps and proposed interpretations. Libraries and conservators safeguarded the fragile originals. Hundreds of contributors worked in an open research community.

No single person, model or machine performed all of those jobs. Nor does a convincing AI-generated image of Greek writing count as an ancient text. The value of this project is precisely that the words can be checked against real physical material.

If you remember one distinction from this story, make it this: the objective was not to manufacture an attractive reconstruction. It was to recover evidence that survived for nearly two thousand years.

## 17 — What could still be hidden?

Researchers estimate that hundreds of Herculaneum rolls remain unopened. Some could preserve additional books by authors we know. Others might contain versions of works thought to be lost, or texts by writers whose names have not survived elsewhere.

But those possibilities are not discoveries yet. We do not know that a lost Aristotle dialogue, a lost poem by Sappho, or an undiscovered history of Rome is waiting inside a particular roll. The evidence is exciting enough without pretending otherwise.

There is another uncertainty: the full extent of the Villa of the Papyri has not been excavated. Scholars have discussed the possibility of more material underground. Future archaeology may clarify what remains, but it would be irresponsible to treat a hoped-for second library as a confirmed treasure vault.

## 18 — The limits of a breakthrough

Even the most advanced scans have limits. Papyrus layers can be missing, folded over themselves or so damaged that their geometry is hard to reconstruct. An ink detector trained on one roll may struggle on another. Higher-resolution scanning takes time and specialized equipment. A recovered Greek sentence may still require years of scholarly debate.

The research is therefore both a technological triumph and a beginning. Reading one surviving core does not mean every unopened roll will yield its words at the push of a button. The work must be repeated carefully, object by object, with transparent evidence and independent checks.

The challenge is to scale without sacrificing accuracy. If it succeeds, the next great discovery may not arrive as a single dramatic headline. It may emerge a few columns at a time, as scholars recover arguments, biographies, scientific ideas and literature that the Roman world once knew.

## 19 — What the volcano left behind

There is something extraordinary about the path these books took. Someone in the ancient world chose them, copied them, carried them into a room and placed them among other works. Then the eruption came. The books became black, delicate remains. Eighteenth-century excavators found them, but could not safely hear all the voices inside.

Modern researchers brought X-rays, algorithms and patient scholarship to the same objects. For the first time, one tightly sealed surviving scroll could be followed through its preserved text, end to end. The recovery did not erase the damage. It made the surviving evidence available for study.

And what did that extraordinary effort reveal? Human beings asking how to live thoughtfully, what we owe to reason and how we might become better people. The questions outlasted the people who wrote them.

## 20 — The last question

Perhaps that is the real secret of Herculaneum. The lost library has not revealed a fantastical history of the ancient world. It has shown us how much of the ordinary intellectual world of antiquity remains absent from our bookshelves — and how a tiny fragment can change what we think we know.

Somewhere among the unopened rolls may be another text no scholar has ever seen. We do not know its title. We do not know its author. But after the breakthrough of June 2026, we finally have a credible way to search without tearing the object apart.

A volcano sealed these books. Centuries of study kept them safe. Now, carefully used technology is helping us read them again.
